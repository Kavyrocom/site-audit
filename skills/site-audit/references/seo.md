# SEO on-page (`seo`)

Sources: `facts.json` (head, headings, links, images, structured data), `crawl/pages.jsonl` and `crawl/summary.json`. For authors, dates and cited sources (items 12 to 15), read the visible text in `rendered.json`: `facts.json` does not extract them.

## Check

1. **Title.** One per page, unique, descriptive, main topic first, brand last. About 30 to 60 characters displays fully on most results (`title_len`); beyond about 65 it is often truncated. The real limit is the width in pixels, not the count of characters (wide letters take more room): treat these numbers as a guide, and check a long title in a search result preview. Duplicate titles across pages (`duplicate_titles`) are a real finding.
2. **Meta description.** Present, unique, 70 to 160 characters, written as a promise with a reason to click. Missing is a low to medium finding (Google may rewrite it); duplicated across many pages is medium.
3. **Headings.** One clear `h1` that says what the page is about and matches the search intent; logical `h2` sections. Several `h1` is a low finding in HTML5 but check that the second one is not a decorative element. No `h1` on a content page is medium. Note that `facts.py` counts `h1` in the raw HTML only.
4. **Search intent match.** Does the page answer the query implied by its title and `h1`? Is the main topic covered in depth compared with what a searcher expects? (`deduced`.)
5. **Content depth.** `thin_content_under_150_words` on pages meant to rank. Thin on a contact or legal page is fine.
6. **Internal links.** Important pages reachable in 3 clicks or fewer (`deep_pages_depth_over_3`), descriptive anchor text (`generic_anchor_text` count: "click here", "read more"), no page with a single inlink that matters (`single_inlink_pages`), no orphans.
7. **Images.** `alt` on meaningful images (`missing_alt_attr` counts missing attributes; `alt=""` is correct for decorative images), modern formats, dimensions set, descriptive file names.
8. **Structured data.** Types present (`jsonld_types`) fit the page: `Organization`/`WebSite` on the home, `Article`/`BlogPosting` on posts, `Product`/`Offer` on products, `FAQPage` only if the questions are visible and you do not promise a rich result for it (since 2023 Google shows FAQ rich results only for well-known government and health sites), `BreadcrumbList` on deep pages. An empty or invalid block is worse than none (check `invalid_jsonld_blocks`). `structured_data.jsonld_missing_required` (and `jsonld_missing_required_properties` in the crawl summary) lists the properties Google documents as required and the block leaves out, for `Product` (and the price of its `Offer`), `BreadcrumbList`, `LocalBusiness` and its common subtypes, `VideoObject` and `Event`. It reports nothing recommended, only what the type cannot do without, and it reads the entities of a block (its root and its `@graph`), not the references inside them. A missing required property means the markup cannot qualify for the rich result: `medium` for a page that sells or is a destination, otherwise `low`. It does not say Google will show the result, or that it is shown today: say so, and name the Rich Results Test as the check for the person. Never recommend markup for content that is not visible on the page.
9. **Social sharing tags.** `og:title`, `og:description`, `og:image` (`open_graph`, `twitter`): they control how links look when shared.
10. **International.** `hreflang` pairs reciprocal and consistent with `html_lang` when the site has several languages.
11. **Keyword and cannibalisation.** Several pages with the same title or `h1` target the same query: pick one, merge or differentiate.
12. **Page type, and what it has to prove.** Google does not judge every page the same way, so name the type before judging. A shop or brand page must look like a credible seller: clear offer, prices, delivery and return terms, reviews. A practitioner or expert page must show real expertise: a named author, credentials, first-hand cases. A testimonial or a discussion page is valued for first-hand experience. A specialist site must show depth across its subject (item 13). Judge each page by what its type has to prove, not against a blog post. (`deduced`.)
13. **Topic coverage.** Google measures topical authority subject by subject, so a site earns it by covering its subject in depth, not with one strong page. In the crawl, look for a main page on a subject with no supporting pages, or many pages on one subject with no page that ties them together and links to them. (`deduced`. No tool measures authority: give no score.)
14. **Proof of expertise on sensitive topics.** For health, money, safety or public matters (Google calls them YMYL), check what a visitor can see: a named author with relevant credentials, sources cited for the claims, a review or update date, who runs the site (about, contact, legal notice). Missing on such a page is `medium` at most and `deduced`. On a recipe or a hobby blog it is not a finding.
15. **Freshness depends on the query.** Update first the pages aimed at news or fast-changing subjects. Elsewhere an old page can rank well: age alone is not a finding. A visible date is expected only on content that ages (news, prices, rules, statistics).

## Traps

- Do not state keyword rankings or search volumes without a data source. Without Search Console or a rank tool, say "not measured".
- `meta keywords` is ignored by Google: not a finding.
- A page can be technically perfect and still not rank because the topic is not wanted: say what you cannot know.
- Google's ranking systems cannot be observed from outside. Describe what the page shows and what its type needs. Never name a Google system as the cause of a ranking, and never give a weight to a signal: public lists of signals say what exists, not what counts most.
